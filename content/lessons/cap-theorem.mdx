## Why this matters

Every distributed database and every "highly available" service you have ever used made a
trade-off described by the **CAP theorem**. Understanding it is the foundation for reasoning
about almost every other topic in this course — replication, consistency models, and failover
strategies all sit on top of it.

## The three properties

- **Consistency (C)** — every read receives the most recent write, or an error. All nodes see
  the same data at the same time.
- **Availability (A)** — every request receives a (non-error) response, without the guarantee
  that it contains the most recent write.
- **Partition tolerance (P)** — the system continues to operate despite an arbitrary number of
  messages being dropped or delayed between nodes.

## The theorem, precisely

> In the presence of a network partition, a distributed system must choose between consistency
> and availability. It cannot provide both.

Network partitions **will** happen — cables get cut, switches fail, packets get dropped. Because
partition tolerance is not really optional for a distributed system, CAP is more useful read as
a choice between **CP** and **AP** during a partition:

| Choice | Behavior during a partition | Example systems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CP** | Reject or block requests that can't be guaranteed consistent | ZooKeeper, etcd, HBase |
| **AP** | Keep serving requests, possibly with stale or conflicting data | Cassandra, DynamoDB, Riak |

## A common misconception

CAP does **not** say "pick any two of three" as a permanent architectural label. Outside of a
partition, a system can be both consistent and available — the trade-off only bites *during* a
partition. This is why later, more nuanced models like **PACELC** extend CAP by also describing
the latency/consistency trade-off that exists even when there is **no** partition.

## Takeaways

1. Partitions are a fact of distributed systems, not an edge case — design for them explicitly.
2. "Consistency" here means **linearizability**, a much stronger guarantee than the "C" in ACID.
3. Most real systems are tunable (e.g., Cassandra's quorum reads/writes) rather than strictly
   CP or AP — CAP is a lens for reasoning, not a rigid checklist.
